How to Read a Company's Claim

Most of what is public about optical AI companies comes from the companies: press releases, product pages and funding announcements. This deck quotes them, links them, and labels each as company claim. A few companies have also published peer-reviewed papers, labelled peer-reviewed. Nothing here has been independently benchmarked by this series.

AskWhy it matters
What is an “operation”?A MAC is usually two operations; some figures count optical operations only, or count an analogue transform as many operations (OptAI 01, slide 05)
At what precision?Each bit doubles converter energy and quadruples photons (OptAI 02, slide 05)
Whose power?Optical power only, the optical chip, or the whole system with memory and host
Measured or projected?“Up to”, “could” and roadmap figures are targets
Against what baseline?Which GPU, at what precision and utilisation, on which workload
On which workload?A selected problem (an Ising instance, a vision task) is not a general AI workload (OptAI 03's scorecard)

Coverage is a snapshot as of October 2026, drawn from public sources found by search; companies not listed may simply have published less. Funding figures are included only where a public announcement states them.

10

The Pull Towards Interconnect

  • A visible pattern: of the companies here that began with optical compute, Lightmatter and Lightelligence now also sell interconnect, and the largest transaction in this list, Marvell's acquisition of Celestial AI, is for optical interconnect (slide 08). Luminous Computing, a photonics start-up that raised $105 million in 2022 to build what it called the world's most powerful AI supercomputer (press release), has made no public announcements since.
  • Why, on the arguments of this series: interconnect moves bits digitally, with no analogue precision to defend, no weights to store, and a clear energy target set by copper and pluggables (OptDC 02). Optical compute must beat a digital MAC whose energy falls every process generation, while paying for conversions, static power and memory (OptAI 03).
  • This is an observation, not a forecast. Compute companies continue to publish and ship, and the peer-reviewed results of 2025 are the most capable optical processors yet shown.
11

Questions to Ask

  1. What workload, at what batch size, and is it a full model or a selected kernel?
  2. What precision at the output, and what accuracy against the digital reference on the same model?
  3. What does the power figure include: optical power, the photonic chip, converters, lasers, memory, host?
  4. How many conversions per MAC: how large is the array, and how often are weights reloaded?
  5. How much static power holds the weights and the calibration, and how well is the engine utilised?
  6. Where do the weights live, and what does fetching them cost?
  7. Measured or projected, and against which digital baseline, at which process node?
  8. Is it peer-reviewed, or independently benchmarked?

Each question maps to a term in the models of OptAI 02 and OptAI 03, and to the interactive on slide 07.

12

What to Take Away

  • Peer-reviewed optical AI systems now exist: Lightmatter's runs ResNet and BERT at near-electronic precision (65.5 trillion operations per second at 78 W plus 1.6 W optical); Lightelligence's solves Ising problems at up to 1 GHz; Microsoft's and Tsinghua's target inference, optimisation and vision.
  • Most large-factor claims (Q.ANT's 30–50×, Lumai's 50×, Neurophos's 100×) are the companies' own, stated in announcements without published methods.
  • Several optical-compute companies have moved towards interconnect, where the engineering case is clearer; Marvell's acquisition of Celestial AI is the largest deal listed.
  • Read every claim with the questions of slide 11, and check it against the conversion floor (the interactive).
  • The series in one line: optics computes linear algebra almost for free, and pays for it in conversions, precision, static power and memory; it wins where those are small against the work done.
